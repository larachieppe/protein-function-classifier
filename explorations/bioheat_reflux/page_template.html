Can a pulse of heat show which way urine flows?

In vesicoureteral reflux (VUR), urine runs backwards from the bladder up the ureter towards the kidney. Today it is diagnosed with X-ray contrast (VCUG) or ultrasound microbubbles (ceVUS). This model asks whether a small temperature tracer could show the direction of flow instead, with no radiation and no contrast agent.

The model

Two ways to use heat as a tracer

The script couples the Pennes bioheat equation in tissue to heat carried by urine in the ureter lumen, solved on an axisymmetric finite-volume grid. Temperatures are rises above core temperature (37 °C).

ρc (∂T/∂t + u·∇T) = ∇·(k∇T) + ωbρbcb(Ta − T) + Q
Protocol A · time-of-flight

Heat a spot, watch both sides

Heat a 1.5 mm spot on the ureter for 5 s, for example with focused ultrasound. Read one thermometry voxel 10 mm towards the kidney and one 10 mm towards the bladder. The side that warms is downstream.

Protocol B · thermal VCUG

Fill the bladder off-temperature

Fill the bladder with saline a few kelvin warmer or cooler than the body, as a VCUG already does with contrast. If urine refluxes during voiding, the temperature anomaly reaches the renal pelvis, where MR thermometry or microwave radiometry can look for it.

Why it can work

Flow beats diffusion by orders of magnitude

The Péclet number compares heat carried by flow with heat that spreads by conduction. Above 1, flow wins. In the ureter it is far above 1 in every regime, so the direction of the warm plume reports the direction of flow almost perfectly. The open question is signal size, not direction.

Heat leaks from urine into the ureter wall in about 5 s. Perfusion clears tissue heat over about 18 min, so it hardly matters on these timescales.

Protocol A · results

Direction is clean. Faster flow gives a weaker signal.

The heat is spread over more urine as flow speeds up, so the downstream rise falls roughly as P/(ρc·Q). Flow also cools the heated spot, which is itself a flow meter but does not give direction.

Protocol A · variant

Heat the still urine first, then wait for the jet

Heating urine that is already moving spreads the heat thin. If the spot is heated while the urine is still, the first reflux jet pushes the whole warm slug past the kidney-side voxel. With only the gentle pulse, that gives a 0.57 K spike on the kidney side and nothing on the bladder side. The spike lasts under a second, so the readout has to sample at about 5 Hz or faster.

Protocol B · results

A thermal VCUG is within reach of MR thermometry

Urine climbing a 12 cm (paediatric) ureter keeps most of its temperature offset. Dilated ureters, which go with worse reflux, keep more. Once pooled in the renal pelvis, the anomaly lasts tens of seconds, which is plenty of time to image.

A cool fill (30–33 °C) is the attractive option: it adds no heat at all. Radiometry fails because its field of view is far larger than the reflux, so the signal is diluted.

Verdict

The physics works. Measuring the temperature is the hard part.

Heat gives an unambiguous direction signal, and the doses needed are far inside safe limits.

Protocol B with MR thermometry looks most practical: a contrast-free MR voiding cystography that only needs a bladder fill a few degrees off body temperature.

Protocol A suits catheter or intra-operative use, where a thermistor or fibre-optic sensor with about 0.05 K noise makes the 0.1–0.5 K plume decisive.

What the model leaves out

  • Motion during voiding corrupts MR (PRF-shift) thermometry. This is probably the biggest practical problem.
  • The ureter is not a steady tube. It collapses between peristaltic boluses, and a collapsed lumen carries no heat.
  • Fat gives no PRF thermometry signal, and the renal sinus is fatty.
  • Kidney cortex perfusion is about 100× the tissue value used here. It matters only if refluxed urine sits against the parenchyma.
  • Contrast ultrasound already avoids radiation. A thermal method would compete on needing no agent at all and on estimating volume.

Next steps
